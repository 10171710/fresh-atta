MillingFrom the journal

Why we still dress our millstones by hand

Every three to four weeks, our two pairs of 750-kilogram granite stones go quiet. We hoist the runner stone using heavy block-and-tackle, wipe away the fine flour film, and set out a set of hardened steel mill picks. What follows is two full days of deliberate, rhythmic tapping: hand-dressing the stones.

Meera Kapoor Head Miller 12 Oct 2026 6 min read

The anatomy of a living stone

A chakki stone is not flat. It is carved with a radial pattern of lands and furrows. The deep valleys (furrows) scoop fresh grain from the eye, funnel cooling air between the stones, and distribute the cracked berries outward. The raised flat plateaus (lands) perform the actual shearing action that rubs the bran and endosperm into microscopic flakes.

Over hundreds of hours of grinding hard whole wheat, the sharp cutting edges of these furrows gradually glaze over and turn slick. When a millstone glazes, it stops cutting cleanly and begins to crush by brute compression. That compression generates destructive heat.

A dull stone cooks flour inside the mill; a sharp, dressed stone glides through grain like scissors through silk.

Why modern machinery cannot replace the mill pick

Visitors frequently ask why we don't dress our stones with electric diamond angle-grinders or computerized laser cutters. The answer comes down to micro-texture:

  1. Micro-grooves over glass glaze: Hand picks create microscopic pitting that grabs the wheat kernel instead of letting it slip and burn.
  2. Graduated furrow depth: Near the eye, furrows must be 12 mm deep to accept whole grain; at the skirt, they taper to less than 1.5 mm to polish the flour. No machine matches a miller's touch for that graduation.
  3. Zero heat stress on the granite: High-speed power tools heat the natural stone locally, which can cause micro-fractures in the crystalline quartz structure.
  4. True balance: Hand-dressing preserves the dynamic balance of the turning stone, preventing harmonic vibration that causes uneven flour fineness.

The thermometer test

The proof of sharp stones is in the palm of your hand. When our stones are freshly dressed, flour leaves the spout at between 34 °C and 38 °C — barely warmer than body temperature. The natural oils inside the germ remain cool and stable, retaining their sweet, grassy perfume and vitamin E content.

When you open your monthly sack of Millstone atta, that buttery aroma is directly attributable to the two days Meera spent with her steel hammers.

How stone-dressing affects your dough

Because hand-dressed stones produce clean, flaked bran rather than pulverized powder, the bran particles have softer edges. They don't sever the gluten strands as you knead your dough, allowing rotis to trap steam and balloon into pillowy, paper-thin phulkas with ease.
